# Learning to Unmix Data: An Introduction to Nonlinear Independent Component Analysis

There are many different ways to represent the information contained in a portrait. For example you could describe features such as hair style, head orientation, facial expression, etc. Alternatively you could start from a representative portrait and then describe deviations. Either way may be used to generate an accurate portrait.

When AI systems generate synthetic portraits, usually the models are under-specified, meaning that they arbitrarily choose factors from which to generate the portraits. The generative factors then do not have interpretable meanings and are dependent on the random initialization of training. Besides lacking interpretability, under-specification also results in unrepeatable training and may even cause a decline in deployment performance [1].

Nonlinear Independent Component Analysis (nICA), a generalization of Independent Component Analysis (ICA), seeks to solve this problem with mathematical guarantees.

Before we get to nICA we should first briefly cover ICA. ICA assumes that your observations $x_i$ were generated by an unknown linear mixing $A$ of unknown sources $s_i$. The goal is to recover $A$ and $s_i$ from $x_i$. In the example above we observe a random mixture of images and recover the original images. Mathematically it is

$$
x_i = A s_i
$$

Putting this into matrix form where each column of $X$ is a sample and each column of $S$ are the sources that generated the sample, ICA solves the matrix factorization

$$
X = AS
$$

However, there are infinitely many solutions to this problem. So ICA makes the assumption that the sources are statistically independent. If you can find a $W = A^{-1}$ such that the rows of $WX$ are statistically independent of each other, you have found the only valid solution to the ICA problem up to the trivial ambiguities of permutation and component-wise scaling. The one caveat is that this is only true if the sources $s$ do not have a Gaussian distribution. That is because for any orthogonal matrix $Q$ and $s \sim N(0,I)$, $Qs \sim N(0,I)$.

Because statistical independence cannot be measured directly from a finite sample, we must approximate it. There are many approximations leading to the plethora of ICA algorithms. The most popular is [FastICA](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.decomposition.FastICA.html).


## Nonlinear ICA

Nonlinear ICA makes the problem even harder by allowing the mixture function to be nonlinear,

$$
x_i = f(s_i)
$$

The goal is to recover $s_i$ by learning a neural network $g$ so that $g(x_i)=s_i$. Note that often the observations have a higher dimension than the sources and so you have to learn both a decoder $f$ and an encoder $g$ so that $f(g(x)) \approx x$. 
Neural networks, like [Variational Autoencoders](https://jaan.io/what-is-variational-autoencoder-vae-tutorial/), have this architecture but without additional constraints cannot recover the sources.
The flexibility in nonlinear functions is such that there are many $g$ where $g(x)$ has an independent non-Gaussian distribution and yet does not recover $s_i$. Consider the figures below. The one on the right shows uniformly distributed sources. The one on the left shows those same sources after a nonlinear transformation. However, the new sources still have an independent uniform distribution, demonstrating the difficulty of this problem.

Nonlinear ICA is still a new field, but several solutions have been proposed. They generally fall into two camps: use auxiliary observations $u_i$ or put restrictions on $f$ and possibly also the distribution of $s$. The first solution is often called weak supervision, where the auxiliary observations could be class labels, time-series indices, or the previous observation for auto-correlated data.

The other solution puts constraints on $f$ such as each source can only influence a subset of the observations or each source must have a sufficiently different influence on the observations. Going back to the portrait example, this would roughly translate to sources controlling unique aspects of the portrait such as hair style, head orientation, or facial expression.

In my research, with smoothness constraints on $f$ and Laplace source distribution, I train a Variation Autoencoder to generate portraits [2]. Because of identifiability guarantees that I proved, the sources were uniquely determined by the data which is a prerequisite to interpretability. It so happens that several of the sources I learned were very interpretable. The gifs below show what happens to the portraits as I vary different sources.

## Summing Up

This brief introduction to nICA hardly scratched the surface of this very deep and important field. A lot of work is still happening to ensure that generative AI systems have a principled framework. The fundamental question is not merely whether a model can generate realistic data, but whether the representation it learns is uniquely determined by the data and assumptions we provide. With these guarantees interpretability becomes much clearer.
